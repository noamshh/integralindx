The Theory Behind IntegralIndx

Understanding E-Graphs, E-Gen embeddings, and the architecture powering mathematical expression similarity search

Why I Built This

Written by Noam Shalev • 2025

In my mathematics research† I spend a lot of time searching for integrals online, both on Google and on Math StackExchange. The frustrating part is that it's almost always textual search. I search for keywords or function names that appear in the integral and hope for the best. If I try to search for the integral itself, unless it's very simple, both Google and MSE struggle to find anything useful.

For years, I had this idea in the back of my mind: build a proper, searchable integral database. But I never had the time, and honestly, my goals were way more ambitious - I wanted a magic machine where you give it an integral and it spits out the closed-form solution. Just like that.*

When I finally had some free time, and after delving deeper into machine learning theory at university, I realized how ridiculously complex this problem actually is. And here's the thing I completely missed: before you can even think about solving integrals automatically, you need good embeddings of mathematical expressions. I never even considered that.

So I started reading some literature. I found MathBERT (Peng et al., 2021), which showed impressive results on mathematical reasoning tasks. The thing is, it's fundamentally a language model. It's trained on mathematical text with surrounding natural language, learning to predict masked tokens based on textual context. That works great when you have narrative around the math, but I needed something that understands pure mathematical equivalence without any surrounding text.

Then I found SemEmb (Gangwar & Kani, 2023), which creates embeddings over symbolic mathematical content directly. This seemed like exactly what I was looking for! It had decent results and was heading in the right direction. But then I stumbled upon the E-Gen (Zheng et al., 2025) paper, and I was genuinely thrilled. E-Gen took everything SemEmb was doing and pushed it much further, using e-graphs, to generate massive synthetic datasets of equivalent expressions.

This demo is my attempt at putting that idea into practice and build a search engine that actually understands mathematical expressions.

E-Graphs & E-Gen Overview

How Do We Get Good Embeddings of Mathematical Expressions?

The key insight is that embeddings need to capture semantic equivalence, not just syntactic similarity. If two expressions are mathematically identical, like sin²(x) + cos²(x) and 1, their embeddings should be close together in vector space. But how do you teach a neural network what "mathematically equivalent" means? The answer is to train it on pairs (or clusters) of equivalent expressions. By showing the model many examples of expressions that mean the same thing but look different, it learns to recognize the underlying mathematical structure rather than just memorizing symbol patterns.

What SemEmb Did

SemEmb took this approach by generating equivalent expression pairs using SymPy's simplification functions. The problem is that SymPy is designed to find the most simplified form directly, without generating intermediate steps or diverse rewrites. This meant SemEmb was limited to cluster sizes of just 2 (pairs of expressions), missing out on the rich diversity of mathematical transformations that exist between the original and simplified forms.

E-Gen's Innovation: E-Graphs

E-Gen solves this limitation using e-graphs (equality graphs), a data structure originally invented in the 1980s by Nelson & Oppen for automated theorem proving. The idea of equality saturation (applying all possible equalities within an e-graph until saturation) was later leveraged for program optimization by Tate et al. (2009). More recently, Willsey et al. (2021) developed the egg library, which made equality saturation fast and practical through new techniques like efficient rebuilding and e-class analyses. Instead of producing just a pair of expressions, equality saturation starts with a seed expression and systematically applies mathematical rewrite rules to explore the entire space of equivalent forms. The e-graph compactly represents all these equivalences in "e-classes", where each e-class contains expressions that are mathematically identical (see this blogpost for a really nice introduction to e-graphs).

This enables E-Gen to generate dozens or even hundreds of equivalent forms per seed expression, creating rich training datasets where each expression becomes a large cluster of mathematically equivalent variants (far more diversity than SemEmb's simple pairs). A transformer trained on these massive clusters using contrastive learning (pulling equivalent expressions together, pushing unrelated ones apart) learns to capture deep semantic relationships rather than surface-level syntactic patterns.

Figure 1: Overview of E-Gen and the cluster-based training framework. Equivalent expressions of each initial expression are generated using an e-graph-based approach, forming clusters of equivalent expressions. The original E-Gen paper implemented both a seq2seq model (trained on equivalent expression pairs) and a contrastive learning model (trained on triplets). For IntegralIndx, I chose to focus on the contrastive learning approach because it's more efficient for similarity search tasks, learning embeddings directly without requiring a decoder.
Source: Zheng et al. (2025), "E-Gen: Leveraging E-Graphs to Improve Continuous Representations of Symbolic Expressions." Figure 2.

The key insight is that expressions like x² + 2x + 1 and (x + 1)² are mathematically identical, and their embeddings should reflect this equivalence. E-graphs provide the machinery to systematically generate and exploit these equivalences during training, going far beyond what simple rewrite rules can achieve.

Data Collection & Processing Pipeline

When I started this project, I thought the data part would be straightforward. Scrape some integrals from Math StackExchange, throw them in a database, and move on to the interesting ML work. Naive me.

Data processing is easily the hardest part of this entire project. Real-world LaTeX from MSE is messy, inconsistent, and filled with edge cases I never imagined. People write integrals in dozens of different ways, use custom macros, nest them in complex equation environments, and mix them with narrative text. I needed to convert all of this dirty, real-world LaTeX into a clean, normalized form that could be reliably parsed. The reason why all that LaTeX cleaning is necessary in the first place is that in order to convert expressions into prefix notation for the transformer, I needed to parse everything through SymPy to get the Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) representation. An AST is essentially a tree structure that captures the mathematical meaning of an expression independent of how it's written. Only with this structured representation could I systematically convert expressions to prefix notation. While there are some good LaTeX-to-SymPy parsers out there, nothing exactly fit my requirements. I ended up forking the latex2sympy2_extended package (which is itself a fork of previous latex2sympy versions) and adding custom parsing capabilities for special functions and integral-specific syntax. Even then, I had to be pretty restrictive: blacklisting certain LaTeX commands that caused parsing issues, setting timeouts on the parser (20 seconds per expression), and rejecting anything that looked remotely ambiguous. I'm sure I lost thousands of potentially valid integrals in the process, but reliability was more important than coverage.

What started as "just scrape and parse" became a multi-stage pipeline with normalization, validation, deduplication, and variable canonicalization. Let me walk you through it.

Pipeline Overview

IntegralIndx's dataset comprises 23,638 integral instances organized into 12,337 unique integrand groups, curated from over 206,000 Math StackExchange expressions. The data processing pipeline ensures mathematical correctness and consistency through multiple validation stages.

Pipeline Stages

Stage 1: Math StackExchange Scraping
  • Source: ~70 pages of MSE data
  • Raw extraction: 206,328 LaTeX expressions
  • Includes both questions and answers with mathematical content
Stage 2: LaTeX Normalization
  • Parse LaTeX environments (align, equation, gather)
  • Extract relation chains (A = B = C → [A, B, C])
  • Capture equivalent forms automatically
  • Separate parametric conditions (e.g., "for x > 0")
Stage 3: Integral Extraction & Validation
  • Advanced integral parsing (definite and indefinite)
  • SymPy validation for mathematical correctness
  • Quality filtering and deduplication
  • Result: 26,730 validated integrals
Stage 4: Variable Normalization & Grouping
  • Standardize integration variables (t, y, θ → x)
  • Normalize unknown functions (f(x), g(x) → UNK_FUNC(x))
  • Canonical parameter ordering (a, b, c → alphabetical)
  • Group by unique integrand hash for deduplication
  • Result: 12,337 unique integrand groups from 23,638 integral instances

Training Process

The model is trained using contrastive learning with the InfoNCE loss objective. The key idea is to pull embeddings of mathematically equivalent expressions closer together in the embedding space while pushing unrelated expressions apart.

Training Objective

  • Task: Learn to embed mathematically equivalent expressions near each other
  • Loss: InfoNCE (contrastive) loss - maximize similarity of positive pairs, minimize for negatives
  • Positive Pairs: Equivalent expressions generated via e-graph rewrites (e.g., sin(x)cos(x) ↔ sin(2x)/2)
  • Negative Pairs: Randomly sampled unrelated expressions from the batch
  • Optimization: Adam optimizer with learning rate scheduling

Data Augmentation with E-Graphs

The power of E-Gen comes from augmenting training data with mathematically equivalent expressions generated using e-graph rewrite rules. This includes:

  • Algebraic simplifications and expansions
  • Trigonometric identities (e.g., sin²(x) + cos²(x) = 1)
  • Logarithmic properties (e.g., log(ab) = log(a) + log(b))
  • Commutativity and associativity rewrites

Figure 2: Training and validation loss curves showing model convergence

By training with contrastive pairs of equivalent forms, the model learns that sin(x)cos(x) and sin(2x)/2 should have similar embeddings (positive pairs), while unrelated expressions like log(x) should be far apart (negative pairs). This enables semantic similarity search beyond mere syntactic matching.

Tokenization & Prefix Notation

Before a mathematical expression can be fed into the transformer model, it must be converted from its standard mathematical notation into a format the model can understand. This involves two key steps: conversion to prefix notation and tokenization. Both are crucial for making the model work with mathematical expressions in a structured, unambiguous way.

Why Prefix Notation?

Prefix notation (also called Polish notation) is a mathematical notation where operators precede their operands. For example, the expression 2 + 3 becomes + 2 3 in prefix notation. This might seem unusual at first, but it has several critical advantages:

  • No operator precedence ambiguity: Unlike infix notation (2 + 3 * 4), prefix notation doesn't require parentheses or precedence rules. + 2 * 3 4 is unambiguous.
  • Tree-based representation: Prefix notation naturally corresponds to the Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) structure of expressions, making it ideal for symbolic computation.
  • Sequential parsing: The model can process tokens left-to-right in a single pass, which is perfect for transformer architectures.
  • E-Gen compatibility: The E-Gen e-graph system requires expressions in S-expression format (Lisp-style notation), which is essentially prefix notation with parentheses.

From SymPy to Prefix to S-expressions

The conversion pipeline follows these steps:

  1. SymPy parsing: User input → SymPy expression object (validated AST)
  2. Prefix notation: SymPy AST → space-separated prefix tokens
  3. S-expression: Prefix tokens → parenthesized Lisp-style notation (for E-Gen)
Example conversions:
SymPy: x**2 + sin(x)
Prefix: add pow x 2 sin x
S-expr: (+ (pow x 2) (sin x))

SymPy: log(x + 1) * a
Prefix: mul log add x 1 a
S-expr: (* (log (+ x 1)) a)

What are S-expressions?

S-expressions (symbolic expressions) are a notation for nested list data, originally from the Lisp programming language. They use parentheses to explicitly mark the boundaries of function applications. E-Gen's e-graph engine uses S-expressions internally because they provide a canonical, unambiguous representation perfect for applying rewrite rules during equality saturation.

Tokenization: Fixed Vocabulary

Once in prefix notation, the expression is split into individual tokens. IntegralIndx uses a frozen vocabulary of exactly 70 tokens, carefully designed to cover the mathematical functions needed for integral expressions while remaining small enough for efficient transformer training.

Vocabulary breakdown (70 tokens total):
  • Variables (4): x (integration variable), a b c (parameters)
  • Constants (14): 0-9 (digits), pi e (mathematical constants), INT+ INT- (multi-digit integers)
  • Operators (49):
    • Basic: add sub mul div pow sqrt
    • Trigonometric: sin cos tan cot sec csc
    • Inverse trig: asin acos atan acot asec acsc
    • Hyperbolic: sinh cosh tanh coth sech csch
    • Inverse hyperbolic: asinh acosh atanh acoth asech acsch
    • Other: exp log abs Li zeta
  • Special tokens (3): PAD SOE EOE (padding, start-of-expression, end-of-expression)

Variable Normalization

To maintain a fixed vocabulary, IntegralIndx performs variable normalization before tokenization:

  • Integration variable: Always normalized to x
    Examples: ∫ f(t) dt → ∫ f(x) dx, ∫ g(y) dy → ∫ g(x) dx, ∫ h(θ) dθ → ∫ h(x) dx
  • Parameters: Ordered alphabetically and renamed to a, b, c (maximum 3 parameters)
    Examples: x^α → x^a, x^β*sin(x) → x^a*sin(x), x^γ*y^δ → x^a*x^b (after y→x)
  • Function whitelist: Only expressions using the 49 supported operators are accepted
Complete example with variable normalization:
Original: ∫₀² y^α · sin(y) dy
After normalization: ∫₀² x^a · sin(x) dx
SymPy canonical: x**a * sin(x)
Prefix notation: mul pow x a sin x
Tokenized: [SOE, mul, pow, x, a, sin, x, EOE, PAD, PAD, ...]

This normalization ensures that mathematically equivalent integrals (differing only in variable names) map to identical token sequences, enabling the model to recognize their semantic similarity.

Model Architecture

IntegralIndx uses an encoder-only transformer architecture based on the original E-Gen paper. The model treats mathematical expressions as sequences in prefix (Polish) notation and learns meaningful embeddings through contrastive learning on mathematically equivalent expressions.

Architecture Specifications

  • Encoder: 6-layer transformer (512 hidden dimensions, 8 attention heads, 0.1 dropout)
  • Feedforward Dimension: 2048 (standard 4× expansion)
  • Embedding Dimension: 512D (mean pooling over sequence)
  • Vocabulary: 70 tokens (see Tokenization section for full breakdown)
  • Positional Encoding: Sinusoidal positional embeddings
  • Pooling Strategy: Mean pooling over token representations

Prefix Notation Representation

Mathematical expressions are converted to prefix notation (Polish notation) for tokenization. This representation:

  • Eliminates ambiguity from operator precedence
  • Provides a canonical sequential form
  • Simplifies parsing and generation
Example transformations:
x² + sin(x) → "add pow x 2 sin x"
log(x + 1) → "log add x 1"
polylog(2, x) → "Li 2 x"

FAISS Similarity Search

Once the transformer model has generated 512-dimensional embeddings for all integrand groups in the database, the next challenge is efficient similarity search: given a query embedding, how do we quickly find the most similar integrands among thousands of candidates? This is where FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search) comes in.

What is FAISS?

FAISS is a library developed by Facebook AI Research for efficient similarity search and clustering of dense vectors. It's optimized for large-scale vector search operations and is widely used in recommendation systems, image retrieval, and natural language processing. FAISS provides both exact and approximate nearest neighbor search algorithms, with support for CPU and GPU acceleration.

Index Type: IndexFlatIP

IntegralIndx uses IndexFlatIP (Flat Inner Product), which performs exact search using inner product (dot product) similarity. Here's why this design works:

  • L2 normalization: All embeddings are L2-normalized to unit length (||v|| = 1)
  • Cosine similarity via dot product: For normalized vectors, the dot product equals cosine similarity:
    cos(θ) = (v₁ · v₂) / (||v₁|| ||v₂||) = v₁ · v₂ (when both are unit vectors)
  • Efficient computation: Inner product is computationally cheaper than explicit cosine calculation
  • No approximation: Flat index guarantees exact nearest neighbors (no false negatives)
Why L2 normalization?

L2 normalization projects all vectors onto the unit hypersphere. In this space, cosine similarity (the angle between vectors) becomes equivalent to the dot product. This transformation allows us to use the efficient IndexFlatIP while measuring semantic similarity based on direction rather than magnitude. Expressions with similar mathematical structure will have embeddings pointing in similar directions, resulting in high cosine similarity.

Search Pipeline

When you submit a query integrand, the system performs the following steps:

  1. Variable normalization: Query → normalize to x, a, b, c (same as training)
  2. SymPy conversion: Normalized query → SymPy AST (validated syntax)
  3. Prefix notation: SymPy AST → space-separated prefix tokens
  4. Tokenization: Prefix tokens → token IDs using 70-token vocabulary
  5. Embedding: Token IDs → 512D embedding vector (via transformer encoder)
  6. L2 normalization: Embedding → unit vector (for cosine similarity)
  7. FAISS search: Find top-k nearest neighbors using inner product
  8. Result formatting: Return integrand groups with similarity scores
Example search:
Query: log(sin(x))
↓ Prefix: log sin x
↓ Embed: [0.023, -0.145, 0.089, ..., 0.201] (512D)
↓ Normalize: ||v|| = 1.0
↓ FAISS search: k=6 nearest neighbors
↓ Results: [log(sin(x)), log(cos(x)), sin(log(x)), ...] with scores [0.95, 0.87, 0.79, ...]

Performance Characteristics

  • Index size: 12,337 vectors × 512 dimensions × 4 bytes = ~25 MB (fits in memory)
  • Search time: Flat index requires O(n) comparisons, but with n=12K this is <10ms on modern CPUs
  • Exact results: No approximation error (unlike IVF or HNSW indexes)
  • Trade-off: For larger datasets (100K+ vectors), approximate indexes like IVFFlat or HNSW would be faster

The combination of contrastive learning, L2-normalized embeddings, and FAISS inner product search creates a powerful semantic similarity engine. Mathematically equivalent expressions (e.g., sin²(x) + cos²(x) and 1) are pulled close together during training, and FAISS efficiently retrieves these neighbors at query time based on their angular proximity in the 512-dimensional embedding space.
