The Theory Behind IntegralIndx

Understanding E-Graphs, E-Gen embeddings, and the architecture powering mathematical expression similarity search

Why I Built This

Written by Noam Shalev • 2025

In my mathematics research† I spend a lot of time searching for integrals online, both on Google and on Math StackExchange. The frustrating part is that it's almost always textual search: I search for keywords or function names that appear in the integral, and hope for the best. If I try to search for the integral itself, unless it's very simple, both Google and MSE struggle to find anything useful.

For years, I had this idea in the back of my mind: build a proper, searchable integral database. But I never had the time, and honestly, my goals were way more ambitious - I wanted a magic machine where you give it an integral and it spits out the closed-form solution. Just like that.*

When I finally had some free time, and after delving deeper into machine learning theory at university, I realized how ridiculously complex this problem actually is. And here's the thing I completely missed: before you can even think about solving integrals automatically, you need good embeddings of mathematical expressions. I never even considered that.

So I started reading some literature. I found MathBERT (Peng et al., 2021), which showed impressive results on mathematical reasoning tasks. The thing is, it's fundamentally a language model. It's trained on mathematical text with surrounding natural language, learning to predict masked tokens based on textual context. That works great when you have narrative around the math, but I needed something that understands pure mathematical equivalence without any surrounding text.

Then I found SemEmb (Gangwar & Kani, 2023), which creates embeddings over symbolic mathematical content directly. This seemed like exactly what I was looking for! It had decent results and was heading in the right direction. But then I stumbled upon the E-Gen (Zheng et al., 2025) paper, and I was genuinely thrilled. E-Gen took everything SemEmb was doing and pushed it much further, using e-graphs, to generate massive synthetic datasets of equivalent expressions.

This demo is my attempt at putting that idea into practice: a search engine that thinks about integrals the way mathematicians do.

E-Graphs & E-Gen Overview

How Do We Get Good Embeddings of Mathematical Expressions?

The key insight is that embeddings need to capture semantic equivalence, not just syntactic similarity. If two expressions are mathematically identical, like sin²(x) + cos²(x) and 1, their embeddings should be close together in vector space. But how do you teach a neural network what "mathematically equivalent" means? The answer is to train it on pairs (or clusters) of equivalent expressions. By showing the model many examples of expressions that mean the same thing but look different, it learns to recognize the underlying mathematical structure rather than just memorizing symbol patterns.

What SemEmb Did

SemEmb took this approach by generating equivalent expression pairs using SymPy's simplification functions. The problem is that SymPy is designed to find the most simplified form directly, without generating intermediate steps or diverse rewrites. This meant SemEmb was limited to cluster sizes of just 2 (pairs of expressions), missing out on the rich diversity of mathematical transformations that exist between the original and simplified forms.

E-Gen's Innovation: E-Graphs

E-Gen solves this limitation using e-graphs (equality graphs), a data structure originally invented in the 1980s by Nelson & Oppen for automated theorem proving. The idea of equality saturation (applying all possible equalities within an e-graph until saturation) was later leveraged for program optimization by Tate et al. (2009). More recently, Willsey et al. (2021) developed the egg library, which made equality saturation fast and practical through new techniques like efficient rebuilding and e-class analyses. Instead of producing just a pair of expressions, equality saturation starts with a seed expression and systematically applies mathematical rewrite rules to explore the entire space of equivalent forms. The e-graph compactly represents all these equivalences in "e-classes", where each e-class contains expressions that are mathematically identical (see this blogpost for a really nice introduction to e-graphs).

This enables E-Gen to generate dozens or even hundreds of equivalent forms per seed expression, creating rich training datasets where each expression becomes a large cluster of mathematically equivalent variants (far more diversity than SemEmb's simple pairs). A transformer trained on these massive clusters using contrastive learning (pulling equivalent expressions together, pushing unrelated ones apart) learns to capture deep semantic relationships rather than surface-level syntactic patterns.

Figure 1: Overview of E-Gen and the cluster-based training framework. Equivalent expressions of each initial expression are generated using an e-graph-based approach, forming clusters of equivalent expressions. The original E-Gen paper implemented both a seq2seq model (trained on equivalent expression pairs) and a contrastive learning model (trained on triplets). For IntegralIndx, I chose to focus on the contrastive learning approach because it's more efficient for similarity search tasks, learning embeddings directly without requiring a decoder.
Source: Zheng et al. (2025), "E-Gen: Leveraging E-Graphs to Improve Continuous Representations of Symbolic Expressions." Figure 2.

The key insight is that expressions like x² + 2x + 1 and (x + 1)² are mathematically identical, and their embeddings should reflect this equivalence. E-graphs provide the machinery to systematically generate and exploit these equivalences during training, going far beyond what simple rewrite rules can achieve.

Model Architecture

IntegralIndx uses an encoder-only transformer architecture based on the original E-Gen paper. The model treats mathematical expressions as sequences in prefix (Polish) notation and learns meaningful embeddings through contrastive learning on mathematically equivalent expressions.

Architecture Specifications

  • Encoder: 6-layer transformer (512 hidden dimensions, 8 attention heads, 0.1 dropout)
  • Feedforward Dimension: 2048 (standard 4× expansion)
  • Embedding Dimension: 512D (mean pooling over sequence)
  • Vocabulary: ~100 tokens (operators, functions, constants, variables)
  • Positional Encoding: Sinusoidal positional embeddings
  • Pooling Strategy: Mean pooling over token representations

Figure 2: Transformer encoder architecture with mean pooling for mathematical expression embeddings

Prefix Notation Representation

Mathematical expressions are converted to prefix notation (Polish notation) for tokenization. This representation:

  • Eliminates ambiguity from operator precedence
  • Provides a canonical sequential form
  • Simplifies parsing and generation
Example transformations:
x² + sin(x) → "add pow x 2 sin x"
log(x + 1) → "log add x 1"
polylog(2, x) → "Li 2 x"

Training Process

The model is trained using contrastive learning with the InfoNCE loss objective. The key idea is to pull embeddings of mathematically equivalent expressions closer together in the embedding space while pushing unrelated expressions apart.

Training Objective

  • Task: Learn to embed mathematically equivalent expressions near each other
  • Loss: InfoNCE (contrastive) loss - maximize similarity of positive pairs, minimize for negatives
  • Positive Pairs: Equivalent expressions generated via e-graph rewrites (e.g., sin(x)cos(x) ↔ sin(2x)/2)
  • Negative Pairs: Randomly sampled unrelated expressions from the batch
  • Optimization: Adam optimizer with learning rate scheduling

Data Augmentation with E-Graphs

The power of E-Gen comes from augmenting training data with mathematically equivalent expressions generated using e-graph rewrite rules. This includes:

  • Algebraic simplifications and expansions
  • Trigonometric identities (e.g., sin²(x) + cos²(x) = 1)
  • Logarithmic properties (e.g., log(ab) = log(a) + log(b))
  • Commutativity and associativity rewrites

Figure 3: Training and validation loss curves showing model convergence

By training with contrastive pairs of equivalent forms, the model learns that sin(x)cos(x) and sin(2x)/2 should have similar embeddings (positive pairs), while unrelated expressions like log(x) should be far apart (negative pairs). This enables semantic similarity search beyond mere syntactic matching.

Data Collection & Processing Pipeline

IntegralIndx's dataset comprises 26,730 integral instances organized into 16,184 unique integrand groups, curated from over 206,000 Math StackExchange expressions. The data processing pipeline ensures mathematical correctness and consistency through multiple validation stages.

Pipeline Stages

Stage 1: Math StackExchange Scraping
  • Source: ~70 pages of MSE data
  • Raw extraction: 206,328 LaTeX expressions
  • Includes both questions and answers with mathematical content
Stage 2: LaTeX Normalization
  • Parse LaTeX environments (align, equation, gather)
  • Extract relation chains (A = B = C → [A, B, C])
  • Handle parametric conditions and multi-statement filtering
  • Identify equivalent forms from equality chains
Stage 3: Integral Extraction & Validation
  • Advanced integral parsing (definite and indefinite)
  • SymPy validation for mathematical correctness
  • Quality filtering: reject piecewise, multi-statement formulas
  • Result: 36,819 valid integrals (44.7% acceptance rate)
Stage 4: Variable Normalization & Grouping
  • Standardize integration variables (t, y, θ → x)
  • Normalize unknown functions (f(x), g(x) → UNK_FUNC(x))
  • Canonical parameter ordering (a, b, c → s, t, u)
  • Group by unique integrand hash for deduplication
  • Result: 16,184 unique integrand groups from 26,730 integral instances
